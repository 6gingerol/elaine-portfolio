Hackathon Judging Portal

A judging system designed to replace spreadsheet-heavy hackathon operations with a connected workflow for assigning judges, reviewing projects, and making award decisions.

TimelineMay 2025 – August 2025
Team4 Designers
3 Developers
RoleProduct Designer
SkillsSystems thinking
Design systems
Interface design

The problem

nwPlus is a student-run organization that runs Western Canada's largest hackathons. With hundreds of participants and projects, judges had to grade hundreds of individual submissions in Google Sheets while organizers managed the whole process by hand. It was inefficient, confusing, and largely repetitive.

From our user discovery sessions with organizers and judges, the same pain points surfaced:

  • ·Clarity — data was scattered across tabs with minimal labelling; only a few people actually understood what was going on.
  • ·Efficiency — judges manually entered scores into formulas across multiple sheets; organizers then had to corroborate all of them together.
  • ·Collaboration — only assigned organizers knew what was going on and what results meant; it was hard to loop anyone else in to help.

This created stress during the most time-sensitive moment of the event, and made it hard to explain why certain projects won.

So we asked

How can we help hackathon organizers and judges turn messy project data into decisions they can trust and act on quickly?

To answer that, we started with pain points and requirements. We sat down with the developers and the logistics team — our main stakeholders and users — to figure out what the system actually had to do and the constraints it needed to work within. With several groups involved in the judging process, we mapped each one into the flow so that different user groups had access to specific actions. As shown in the figure: white is all users, red is external judges, purple is external and internal judges (admin), and blue is collaborators.

My scope

The team designed the full end-to-end flow together: from event setup and rubric creation to assigning judges, scoring, and awards. Within that system, my focus was the Statistics Viewer — where organizers go once judging is underway to:

  • ·Create or edit awards with their criteria scores
  • ·See project rankings and data for a specific award
  • ·Review projects and look for red flags

My goal: make reviewing projects and locking in winners feel clear and trustworthy, even when the underlying data is still incomplete or changing.

Design insights

During the early design stage, I ran group walkthroughs with 4 target users to validate the task flows. I wanted to see whether an organizer's mental model of reviewing projects for an award matched the statistics workflow I had designed.

Originally we had a simple orange flagging system, where judges could flag a project, lock in a winner, and filter for flagged projects. But the walkthroughs surfaced two problems:

  • ·The system for selecting winners and problematic projects felt too flat.
  • ·There was no clear path from "I like this project" to "this is a winner."

Judges needed more room to build trust before making a call — so the single flag gave way to a multi-step review system.

On the visual side, the signifiers for flagging a project and choosing a winner weren't working as intended. A flagged project needed more distinction so organizers could tell at a glance that it was problematic — and the reason it was flagged mattered too, so I added flag notes. We also realized some awards could have more than one winner, so I added a multiple-winner action.

The shipped portal

The result is a Statistics Viewer that lets organizers move through awards one by one, see ranked projects with their scores, catch anything that needs a second look, and finalize decisions in the same place. No exporting, no cross-referencing sheets.

Reflection

Designing for data that's incomplete and constantly changing taught me to design for the messy middle, not just the finished state. A view that looks great with perfect data falls apart the moment a score is missing, so the real work was making "not done yet" feel just as clear as "done."
